\title{A Held-Out Audit of Nonlinear Filters for\\
Light-Level Geolocation of a Juvenile White Shark}

\begin{abstract}
Pop-up archival transmitting (PAT) tags infer animal position from the timing of
sunrise and sunset. The measurement is well conditioned in longitude and nearly
singular in latitude near an equinox, which makes it a useful stress test for
nonlinear filtering. I build an extended Kalman filter, an unscented Kalman
filter, a bootstrap particle filter, two smoothers and a grid HMM for a 137-day
juvenile white shark (\emph{Carcharodon carcharias}) record, and I score them
against 122 Argos satellite fixes obtained for the same animal.
The contribution is not an error number; it is an audit. I hold out the final
30 days of the ground truth, never using it for any modelling or tuning
decision, and I repeat every stochastic run over ten seeds.
Eight results follow. First, the best configuration I built reaches
$117$\,\si{\km} median error on the window I tuned on and $544$\,\si{\km} on the
held-out window, where the proprietary GPE3 smoother reaches
$118$\,\si{\km}: the headline was a tuning-window number.
Second, seed-to-seed spread is $29$--$90$\,\si{\km}, far larger than the
$1$--$5$\,\si{\km} steps an earlier ablation ladder reported, so those steps were
noise. Third, across $75$ pipeline variants, tuning-window rank predicts
held-out rank only weakly (Spearman $\rho=+0.30$), and the variant that looks
best while tuning ranks $54$th of $75$ when finally tested. Fourth, the failure
has an identifiable physical cause, and a one-parameter fix chosen without ever
touching the held-out data raises tuning-window $1\sigma$ coverage from
$22\%$ to $38\%$ while leaving held-out error unchanged to worse. Fifth, and the reason the fix could not have worked, a threshold
bias is confounded with position by construction: a twilight pair is two numbers
and position is two unknowns, so one minute of common-mode bias is
observationally identical to $25$\,\si{\km} of longitude and one minute of
antisymmetric bias to $112$\,\si{\km} of latitude. Widening the bias family to
three dimensions and reapplying the same marginal-likelihood rule selects the
degenerate direction every time and makes tuning-window error four times worse.
Evidence is maximised exactly where the model is least identifiable. Sixth, a particle smoother -- the one repair the identifiability result does not forbid, since it adds no parameter -- cuts tuning-window error from $119$ to $69$\,\si{\km} but leaves the held-out error statistically unchanged at $388\pm23$ against $404\pm3$\,\si{\km}, confirming from the estimator side that what remains is bias and not variance. Seventh, auditing the temperature channel produced a defect rather than an improvement: the quarter-degree sea-surface-temperature analysis returns no value at the animal's own Argos fix on $20\%$ of the days the term fires, and the likelihood mapped those cells to zero probability, deleting the truth; repairing it moves tuning-window mean error from $167$ to $144$\,\si{\km} and the $75$th percentile from $273$ to $191$\,\si{\km} while making the median $20$\,\si{\km} worse, because the repair rotates the error rather than shrinking it. Eighth, the two remaining knobs behave in opposite
ways. The process-noise scale was fitted on the one month the animal was
migrating and is $2.4$ times too large for the rest of the record; halving it
is selected on tuning data alone and cuts held-out median error from $412$ to
$313$\,\si{\km}. A weak seafloor-depth habitat prior, by contrast, looks like
the largest single held-out gain in the study and is then beaten by a
deliberately inverted copy of itself: across seven such priors what predicts
the gain is how much ocean each one deletes ($r = +0.95$), not where it
points. Ninth, applying the frozen configuration to six further double-tagged
animals from the same public archive first exposed two defects that no
single-animal study can reveal: the seafloor constraint was keyed to the tuned
animal's timestamps and silently disabled itself on every other shark, and the
filter's clock was anchored to the tuned animal's release date, which left one
animal's filter frozen on its release prior for its entire record and gave it
the lowest error in the study. With both repaired, halving the process noise
helps all seven animals, by $8$ to $32\%$ in median error, with the tuned
animal's own numbers unchanged. The only estimator that holds up on both windows is the proprietary one I did
not write, which I report here as the benchmark to beat.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A PAT tag carries no receiver. It records light, pressure and temperature, and
position must be inferred afterwards from the astronomy of the light curve.
Local apparent noon gives longitude directly and robustly. Latitude comes only
from day length, and
$\partial(\text{day length})/\partial(\text{latitude}) \rightarrow 0$ at an
equinox, so for weeks around it the inverse problem is locally rank deficient in
latitude. This deployment runs straight through the March 2008 equinox.

That makes it a good test case for nonlinear filtering, and the second tag on
the same animal makes it a rare one: the Argos fixes give an independent answer
that the light data never sees. Most light-level geolocation papers validate
against simulation or against a single reference track. Here there is a real,
external, irregularly sampled ground truth.

This paper is deliberately an audit rather than a method paper. An earlier
version of this pipeline reported a best median error near $119$\,\si{\km} and a
ladder of incremental improvements. Everything in Section~\ref{sec:proto} was
added afterwards, and most of what the ladder claimed did not survive it. The
useful output is therefore a set of statements about what the data can and
cannot resolve, and a short list of process errors that produced the original
over-claim.

\section{Data}
\subsection{Tag record}
Deployment \texttt{07\_05} is a juvenile white shark tagged off central
California on 5 February 2008. The archival tag was physically recovered, so the
full-resolution record is available rather than the summarised transmission:
light level, pressure and temperature at $10$-second to $1$-minute cadence over
$137$ days. The animal left the shelf and moved south and offshore, ending near
the southern Gulf of California.

Twilight events are extracted in two ways. The manufacturer's LightLoc
processing yields $251$ twilight times, $241$ after quality control. A
re-extraction directly from the raw light curves yields $228$ events with an
explicit depth of detection attached to each, which Section~\ref{sec:recal}
uses.

\subsection{Ground truth}
A SPOT tag on the same animal returned $122$ Argos fixes between
15 February and 15 June 2008. Location classes are $3$ ($n=3$), $2$ ($20$),
$1$ ($42$), $0$ ($24$), A ($16$) and B ($16$). I assign them nominal
one-sigma radii of $0.25$, $0.5$, $1.5$, $10$, $3$ and $9$\,\si{\km}
respectively; classes 0, A and B are unquoted by Argos, so these are the
empirical marine-vertebrate values rather than anything the system guarantees.

The Argos track is the only ground truth used anywhere in this paper. It is
never an input to any estimator.

\section{Observation and Motion Models}
\subsection{Twilight timing}
For a twilight event at time $t$ of kind $k \in \{\text{dawn},\text{dusk}\}$, the
predicted event time is obtained by solving for the instant at which the solar
elevation equals a threshold $h_0^{(k)}$. The threshold is not the geometric
horizon: it absorbs sensor response, cloud, and the fact that the tag may be at
depth when the threshold is crossed. I model it as
\begin{equation}
h_0^{(k)}(z) = h_0^{(k)} + \beta \log(1+z),
\label{eq:h0}
\end{equation}
with $z$ the depth in metres at which the twilight was recorded. The residual is
taken in minutes and given a Student-$t$ likelihood with $\nu=4$ degrees of
freedom, which matters more than any other modelling choice here
(Section~\ref{sec:matched}).

Two calibrations of \eqref{eq:h0} appear in this paper. The first is frozen on the
LightLoc times in the calibration window and gives
$h_0^{\text{dawn}}=-9.91\degree$, $h_0^{\text{dusk}}=-0.29\degree$ and
$\beta=+0.89$ per log-metre, with per-branch residual standard deviations of
$12.88$ and $10.43$ minutes. The second refits the same form on the raw-light
times and gives $h_0^{\text{dawn}}=-5.56\degree$,
$h_0^{\text{dusk}}=-5.88\degree$ and $\beta=+0.0085$, with a residual RMS of
$4.80$ minutes. The second looks much better and behaves much worse;
Section~\ref{sec:recal} is about that.

\subsection{Sea-surface-temperature aiding}
Daily $0.25\degree$ optimally interpolated SST is compared with the tag's
shallowest daily temperature, and the resulting log-likelihood is added to the
twilight term. The point of SST is not that it is accurate. It is that its
information is orthogonal to day length, so it carries latitude through the
equinox where the light data cannot. In March alone it moves the particle
filter from $522$ to $109$\,\si{\km} median error.

\subsection{Seafloor-depth aiding}
Where the tag's maximum daily depth exceeds the ETOPO seafloor depth at a
candidate position, that position is impossible. This enters as
$\log\Phi\!\left((d_{\max}(p)-z)/\sigma_B\right)$ with $\sigma_B=150$\,\si{\m},
plus a finite land penalty. It is a hard, highly informative constraint, and it
makes the posterior strongly multimodal, which is why the choice of point
estimate stops being innocent.

\subsection{Motion model}
The state is $x=[\varphi,\lambda,v_E,v_N]$ with a damped random walk in velocity,
\begin{equation}
\dot v = -v/\tau + w, \qquad \tau = 3\ \text{days},
\end{equation}
and $\sigma_v$ set so the stationary speed distribution has a sensible mean for
a juvenile white shark. Positions are confined to a generous box
($15\degree$--$50\degree$\,N, $140\degree$--$105\degree$\,W).

\section{Estimators}
I implement, all on the same observation and motion models: a naive
event-by-event inversion as a floor; an EKF and a UKF with $3\sigma$ innovation
gating; Rauch--Tung--Striebel and unscented RTS smoothers for each; a bootstrap
particle filter with systematic resampling and roughening; a two-filter particle
smoother; a cloud-level fusion of two particle filters differing only in their
observation model; and a grid HMM over a $0.25\degree$ lattice. The proprietary
GPE3 hidden-Markov smoother supplied by the tag manufacturer is included as an
external reference; I did not implement it and cannot inspect it.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:proto}
This section is the part of the paper I would read first, because every number
after it depends on it and none of it existed in the earlier version.

\subsection{Three windows}
The record is split once, by date, and never re-split:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Calibration}, up to 10 March 2008. The only window in which any
sensor parameter is fitted.
\item \textbf{Tuning}, 11 March to 15 May 2008. Every modelling decision,
hyperparameter and readout choice in this paper was made by looking at this
window. It contains $64$ truth-matched twilight events.
\item \textbf{Lockbox}, the final 30 days from 16 May 2008. Scored once per
configuration, after the configuration was frozen. It contains $20$ events.
\end{itemize}
Nothing is selected on the lockbox anywhere, including the one hyperparameter
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:fix}. Where a lockbox column appears next to a
selection rule, it is printed for the reader and was not available to the rule.

\subsection{Metrics}
Error is the great-circle distance between an estimate interpolated to an Argos
fix time and that fix, reported as a median over matched events; the median
rather than the mean because the naive inversion produces occasional
thousand-kilometre outliers that would otherwise dominate. Alongside it I report
$1\sigma$ coverage: the fraction of matched events at which the truth falls
inside the estimator's own reported standard deviation. Nominal coverage for a
Gaussian is $68\%$. An estimator that is wrong is usable; an estimator that is
wrong and confident is not.

\subsection{Monte Carlo repetition}
Every particle-based result is the mean over ten seeds, reported with its
standard deviation across seeds. This is the single cheapest change in the
paper and the one that invalidated the most prior conclusions.

\subsection{The headline was a tuning-window number}
The earlier version of this work quoted its best configuration at roughly
$119$\,\si{\km} and stopped there. That number survives re-running: the
two-filter cloud fusion scores $117.4$\,\si{\km} on the full evaluation window.
What does not survive is the interpretation. On the final 30 days the same
configuration scores $543.9$\,\si{\km}, against $118.4$\,\si{\km} for GPE3 on
exactly those days. The full-window figure is dominated by the tuning window,
which carries $64$ of the $84$ matched events, so quoting it as an accuracy is
close to quoting a training error.

\subsection{Seed noise sets the resolution floor}
Table~\ref{tab:head} repeats one configuration ten times at $120{,}000$
particles. The standard deviation across seeds is $29$\,\si{\km} on the full
window and $90$\,\si{\km} on the lockbox. The earlier ablation ladder reported
improvements of $1$--$5$\,\si{\km} per step and treated them as real. They are
an order of magnitude below this floor. Measuring the noise before building the
ladder would have removed most of the ladder.

\subsection{Tuning-window skill barely transfers}
\label{sec:transfer}
Every pipeline variant I built was scored on both windows: $75$ configurations,
spanning filters, smoothers, aiding combinations, readouts and particle counts.
Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer} plots lockbox error against tuning error for all of
them. The Spearman rank correlation is $\rho=+0.30$ ($p=8.8\times10^{-3}$).

A weak positive correlation is worse news than it sounds. It means tuning-window
rank carries some information about held-out rank, but far too little to select
on. Concretely: the configuration that looks best while tuning
($84.9$\,\si{\km}) comes $54$th of $75$ on the lockbox, and the configuration
that is actually best on the lockbox ($319.4$\,\si{\km}, an EKF smoother on
QC'd events) comes $40$th of $75$ on tuning. Picking the tuning-window winner is
close to picking at random, and because the search was large, the winner is
mostly the variant that best fitted the noise in $64$ events.

\subsection{The filters do not know they are wrong}
Coverage is where the failure is least ambiguous. In Table~\ref{tab:head} the
published configuration attains $22\%$ $1\sigma$ coverage on the tuning window
and $9\%$ on the lockbox, against a nominal $68\%$. In the lockbox it reports a
median standard deviation of $201$\,\si{\km} while being wrong by
$532$\,\si{\km} --- roughly a $2.6\sigma$ systematic offset, sustained over 30
days, reported as if it were a $1\sigma$ situation.

Under-coverage this severe is not a tuning problem. A filter whose process noise
is merely too small produces errors that are too large relative to its variance
in a random direction. Here the error has a consistent sign in latitude, which
points at the measurement model rather than at the filter.

\subsection{A matched comparison of EKF, UKF and PF}
\label{sec:matched}
The particle filter beats the Kalman filters in Table~\ref{tab:main}, but it
also differs from them in two ways at once: it uses a Student-$t$ likelihood and
it enforces the feasibility box. Table~\ref{tab:ablate} separates these by
running all four combinations through the same particle filter code path.

The heavy tails are worth $101$\,\si{\km}; the box is worth $9$\,\si{\km}. A
Gaussian particle filter is $84$\,\si{\km} \emph{worse} than the EKF, despite
representing the posterior far more faithfully than a covariance can. So the
honest statement is not that the particle filter beats the Kalman filters. It is
that a Student-$t$ likelihood beats a Gaussian one, and either filter can have
that. The non-Gaussian posterior was not the point.

\section{Diagnosis}
\subsection{The residual at the true position}
Because the Argos fixes exist, I can do something better than inspect filter
output: I can evaluate the measurement model at the \emph{true} position. For
each twilight event with an Argos fix within $8$ hours ($63$ of $228$ events), I
compute the predicted twilight time at the interpolated true position and
subtract the observed one. This isolates measurement-model error from filter
error completely.

Table~\ref{tab:resid} gives the result by half-month. Two features matter. The
branch-corrected residual $y$ sits near $+34$ minutes from the first half of
March onwards, which is five to six times the model's own $\sigma$; and the two
twilight branches move in \emph{opposite} directions, with lockbox medians of
$+12$ minutes at dawn and $-71$ minutes at dusk.

The antisymmetry is the diagnostic. A clock error, or a drift in the tag's time
base, would shift both branches the same way. An antisymmetric shift compresses
the apparent day length, and day length is exactly what carries latitude. At
this time of year about $60$ minutes of day-length error is worth roughly
$650$\,\si{\km} of latitude, which matches the size and the sign of the lockbox
latitude bias of $-413$ to $-543$\,\si{\km} measured in Table~\ref{tab:ladder}.

So the model was not slightly wrong at the end of the deployment. It was wrong
by five to six of its own sigmas for the whole record, and the lockbox merely
made that visible.

\subsection{Depth of detection, and an unidentifiable parameter}
The depth at which a twilight is recorded is the obvious candidate mechanism:
light attenuates with depth, so a tag deeper in the water column crosses any
fixed irradiance threshold later at dawn and earlier at dusk --- antisymmetric,
as observed.

The depth distribution differs sharply between windows. In the calibration
window the median twilight depth is $51$\,\si{\m} and the 95th percentile is
$94$\,\si{\m}; in the lockbox the median is $98$\,\si{\m} and the 95th percentile
is $182$\,\si{\m}. The parameter $\beta$ in \eqref{eq:h0} was therefore fitted
over a range of depths that the lockbox almost entirely exceeds.

Quantitatively the mechanism is consistent but weakly evidenced. Regressing
$y$ on $\log(1+z)$ over the non-lockbox events gives
$y \approx 8.8 + 4.9\log(1+z)$, and extrapolating that to the lockbox median
depth of $98$\,\si{\m} predicts $+31.5$ minutes against $+27.5$ observed. The
magnitude is right. But the rank correlation itself is weak and not significant
($\rho=+0.27$, $p=0.05$, $n=51$), so I would call the mechanism consistent with
the data rather than established by it.

The sharper statement is about identifiability. Fitted on the raw-light twilight
times, $\beta$ comes out at $+0.0085$ per log-metre, essentially zero. Fitted on
the LightLoc times, the same physical quantity comes out at $+0.89$. Two orders
of magnitude apart. No goodness-of-fit diagnostic in the calibration window
flags this, because the calibration window does not span enough depth to
constrain $\beta$ at all. A support check does flag it, and costs nothing.

\subsection{A recalibration that made things worse}
\label{sec:recal}
This is the single most useful negative result here, and the easiest to state.

Refitting \eqref{eq:h0} on the raw-light twilight times cuts the
calibration-window residual RMS to $4.80$ minutes, against per-branch standard
deviations of $12.88$ and $10.43$ minutes for the original LightLoc calibration.
By every calibration-window diagnostic it is a large improvement.

Held out, it is a large regression. On identical events, the identical filter and
the identical seed, the refitted constants give $364.4$\,\si{\km} median error on
the evaluation window where the original constants give $124.3$\,\si{\km}. A
recalibration that more than halves the residual it was fitted to makes
out-of-sample accuracy roughly three times worse.

The mechanism is the one above: the refit buys its low residual by driving
$\beta$ to zero, which is defensible inside a window spanning $0$--$94$\,\si{\m}
and indefensible in a window reaching $182$\,\si{\m}. The general lesson is that
a sensor-error model fitted on a window that does not span the operating range of
its own regressors will look well calibrated on that window and fail silently
outside it.

A practical note belongs here too. In the notebook these constants live at module
scope and are installed by a context manager, because the first version of this
audit ran the raw-light filters outside that scope and silently used the wrong
constants --- producing numbers that looked three times better than they were.
The audit now begins with an equivalence test asserting that the bias-state
filter with $\sigma_b=0$ reproduces the published filter to within seed noise
($-8.3$ and $-6.4$\,\si{\km} on the two readouts). Global mutable calibration
state is a reproducibility hazard, and a cheap assertion catches it.

\section{A Fix Chosen Without the Lockbox}
\label{sec:fix}
The diagnosis says the threshold is biased and antisymmetric. Rather than try to
predict that bias offline --- which Section~\ref{sec:recal} shows is exactly
where the previous attempt failed --- the filter can estimate it online. I add
one scalar to the state, a threshold bias $b$ in minutes entering the
observation as $+b$ at dawn and $-b$ at dusk, evolving as a random walk with
scale $\sigma_b$ per $\sqrt{\text{day}}$. The state becomes
$[\varphi,\lambda,v_E,v_N,b]$. This adds one hyperparameter and no new data.

\subsection{Selecting $\sigma_b$ honestly}
$\sigma_b$ is chosen by maximising the filter's own marginal likelihood
accumulated over the tuning window only. Table~\ref{tab:sigb} shows the sweep.
The evidence criterion selects $\sigma_b=3$. For comparison, a rule that matched
tuning-window coverage to nominal would have selected $\sigma_b=1$, and the
value that is actually best on the lockbox is $\sigma_b=5$ --- which the
evidence rule did not pick, as it should not, having never seen the lockbox.

Of the six non-zero values, all six beat $\sigma_b=0$ on tuning-window evidence,
two beat it on lockbox median error, and two beat it on lockbox coverage. The
evidence criterion reliably prefers a bias state; the held-out data does not
reliably reward one.

\section{Why the Fix Could Not Have Worked}
\label{sec:ident}

Section~\ref{sec:fix} reports a bias state that improves calibration and not
accuracy, and I wrote that up as a fix that failed. That is the right description
of the measurement and the wrong diagnosis. The bias state could not have worked,
and the reason is structural rather than empirical.

\subsection{The bias is confounded with position by construction}
A day of twilight data gives exactly two numbers, a dawn time and a dusk time.
Position has exactly two unknowns. The measurement Jacobian
$J = \partial(t_\text{dawn}, t_\text{dusk}) / \partial(\varphi, \lambda)$ is
therefore square, and generically invertible away from the equinox. Any additional
parameter that enters through those same two numbers lies in the range of $J$: it
is a linear combination of a latitude shift and a longitude shift, and no quantity
of twilight data can separate it from one. This is not a weak-identifiability
statement that more data would fix. It is exact.

The only free question is the exchange rate, which follows from solving
$J\,\delta = e$ for the position displacement $\delta$ that reproduces a given
observation perturbation $e$. A common-mode bias is $e = (+1, +1)$ minutes and an
antisymmetric bias is $e = (+1, -1)$. Table~\ref{tab:ident} evaluates this at the
interpolated Argos positions, so the rates are physical.

configuration scores $1165$\,\si{\km} on the tuning window against $274$\,\si{\km}

($p = 4.8 \times 10^{-19}$), and between $\log Z_\text{tune}$ and tuning-window
coverage it is $\rho = -0.57$. Higher evidence reliably means worse.

The mechanism is not subtle once stated. Marginal likelihood rewards explaining the
observations. A degenerate direction explains observations perfectly, by absorbing
whatever residual is left, and pays nothing for it because the data cannot
contradict it. So evidence is maximised exactly where the model is least
identifiable.

\subsection{The filter doing it, in one number}
Running the selected configuration and correlating the estimated common-mode bias
against the signed longitude error gives Spearman $\rho = +0.94$. The filter is not
measuring a property of the sensor. It is relabelling its own longitude error as a
threshold offset, and scoring itself well for the relabelling.

\subsection{Consequences}
Three, in increasing order of how much they cost me.

The one-parameter fix of Section~\ref{sec:fix} is not a fix that failed, it is a
parameter that was never estimable from twilight data. Its coverage gain is real
but it is the honest widening of an interval around an unidentifiable quantity, not
an improvement in knowledge.

Selection hygiene is necessary and not sufficient. A rule that never sees held-out
data can still be the wrong rule, and mine was: it was safe in
Table~\ref{tab:sigb} only because the family was one-dimensional and small. Widen
the family and the same rule walks straight into the degenerate direction. I would
now pair any evidence-based selection with an identifiability check on the
parameters being selected over.

What breaks the degeneracy is information that does not arrive through the twilight
pair. That is exactly what the SST and seafloor terms are, and it explains why they
are worth more here than any filter, smoother or likelihood choice. The productive
direction for this record was never a better estimator. It was a third independent
measurement.

\section{Smoothing: Variance Versus Bias}
\label{sec:smooth}

Section~\ref{sec:ident} closes off the measurement-model repairs: any extra
parameter that enters through a dawn/dusk pair is already in the span of
$(\varphi,\lambda)$. Smoothing is the one remaining idea it does not touch,
because it adds no parameter at all. It only insists that the estimate at time
$t$ use the observations after $t$ as well as those before.

The prediction is worth stating before the numbers, because it is the whole
point. A smoother removes variance and lag. It cannot remove a bias in the
measurement model: if every twilight reports the same distortion, propagating
that information backwards as well as forwards reports the distortion twice. So
a large gain is expected on the tuning window, where the Argos fixes are dense
and the residual error is mostly spread, and little or none on the lockbox,
where Section~\ref{sec:ident} says the error is structural.

\subsection{Two smoothers, and three things checked before trusting either}

I implement both a forward-filtering backward-simulation smoother (FFBS) and the
generalised two-filter smoother. They approximate the same marginals by
different routes, so agreement between them is evidence and disagreement is a
bug.

The forward pass is the configuration of Section~\ref{sec:fix}: hard seafloor
set, SST aiding, $12\,000$ particles. To store its full state cloud I patch the
source of the published filter rather than retyping its loop, so the two cannot
drift apart; the resulting track is bit-identical to the published one (maximum
absolute difference $0$). The transition density used backwards is checked
against $150\,000$ simulated draws of the forward propagation and agrees to
$3.6\times10^{-15}$.

For the two-filter smoother the artificial prior on velocity is taken to be the
stationary law of the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck velocity and the backward proposal its
exact time reversal, which makes the importance ratio
$\gamma_t(x_t)f(x_{t+1}\mid x_t)/[\gamma_{t+1}(x_{t+1})q(x_t\mid x_{t+1})]$
identically one. The code measures that ratio rather than trusting the algebra:
the largest absolute log ratio over all $241$ steps is $1.3\times10^{-12}$.
Fusion is done between the particle clouds, not between Gaussian summaries of
them.

\subsection{A large gain on the tuning window, none on the lockbox}

Table~\ref{tab:smooth} gives the result over five seeds. On the tuning window
the smoother cuts median error from $119.0\pm1.4$ to $68.6\pm4.4$\,\si{\km}, a
gap of $50.3$\,\si{\km} at $10.9$ standard errors, and the geometric-median
readout reaches $56.8\pm3.8$\,\si{\km}. This is the largest single improvement
in the study, and it costs no new parameter and no new data.

On the lockbox the same smoother moves the error from $404.2\pm2.9$ to
$388.2\pm22.8$\,\si{\km}, a gap of $0.7$ standard errors, which is not a
difference. The signed error is almost unchanged: $378$\,\si{\km} too far south
becomes $368$\,\si{\km} too far south. The two smoothers also agree with each
other to about $1$\,\si{\km} on both windows despite sharing no backward
machinery, which is the strongest single argument that the number is real.

Three controls support the reading. The answer is flat in the smoothing budget
($66.5$--$67.3$\,\si{\km} for $K$ from $100$ to $1600$ trajectories), so it is
not a compute artefact. None of the $96\,400$ smoothed states leaves the hard
seafloor set, so admissibility survives smoothing by construction rather than by
luck. And the posterior spread falls to $0.65$ of the filter's while $95\%$
coverage on the tuning window \emph{rises} from $92.2\%$ to $93.8\%$: the
intervals get narrower and more honest at the same time. On the lockbox coverage
is $37.5\%$ before and after, which is the signature of an interval centred in
the wrong place rather than one that is too narrow.

One stated motivation does not survive. The reason usually given for smoothing
here is lag, and there is very little lag to remove: shifting the filter's track
in time buys only $1.6$\,\si{\km}, at a best shift of one day. The improvement
comes from using future observations to sharpen each marginal.

\subsection{A reason I had skipped this, and why it was wrong}

An earlier step in the notebook rejected backward simulation on an explicit
calculation: the position process noise over a median step is about
$2$\,\si{\km} while the posterior cloud is about $125$\,\si{\km} across, so only
$\sim3.5$ of $12\,000$ particles lie within one standard deviation of a backward
target. That arithmetic is correct and I reproduce it: the median effective
number of ancestors per target is $2.9$.

The conclusion drawn from it was not. Two claims were conflated. That the
backward weights \emph{underflow} is a statement about floating point, and it
disappears once the weights are formed in log space with the row maximum
subtracted. That the sampler \emph{collapses} does not follow at all, because
each trajectory draws its own ancestor independently; a concentrated weight
vector pins one trajectory rather than collapsing the ensemble. Measured
directly, $306$ of $400$ smoothed trajectories remain distinct at the start of
the record, and Table~\ref{tab:smooth} shows the ensemble is better calibrated
than the filter it came from. Rejecting the method on that argument cost roughly
$50$\,\si{\km} of tuning-window accuracy.

\section{Temperature: Two Accidental Corrections}
\label{sec:sst}

Section~\ref{sec:ident} closes the twilight channel and Section~\ref{sec:smooth}
shows that a smoother removes variance but not bias. The one remaining place to
look for independent information is the thermometer. The tag reports a surface
temperature with each dawn, and the pipeline compares it to the NOAA OISST
quarter-degree daily analysis through a Gaussian likelihood with one fitted bias
and one fitted standard deviation, thinned to every third day to respect the
analysis error correlation.

Two files in the same archive had never been opened: a binned depth--temperature
profile roughly every two hours, and a mixed-layer summary. Neither supplies a
second position fix. What they supply is a per-day statement of how far the one
temperature reading can be trusted, and using them turned up a defect rather
than an improvement.

\subsection{The land mask was deleting the true position}

Evaluating the residual at the \emph{true} Argos position removes the position
error and leaves the observation model exposed on its own. Done that way, the
first thing visible is not a noise-model problem at all. On $19$ of the $95$ dawn
events that carry both a temperature reading and a defensible truth---one fifth
of them, and $14$ of the $18$ that fall in the lockbox---OISST returns no value
at the animal's own Argos fix. Seventeen of those nineteen are water according to
the same bathymetry the seafloor constraint already uses, at a median depth of
\SI{103}{\metre}. They lie in the Gulf of California, which is narrower than the
cell the analysis is built on.

The likelihood mapped a missing reference to $-\infty$, reasoning that the animal
cannot be on land. A gap in OISST does not mean land; it means land to a
quarter-degree product. Averaged over sampled positions on days when the term
fires, it was assigning zero probability to $35.3\%$ of the map, the true
position included. Nothing is lost by treating a missing reference as
uninformative instead: the hard seafloor set of Section~\ref{sec:fix} already
rejects land exactly, at far finer resolution, and it is the term that is
supposed to be doing that job.

\subsection{The tag measures its own reliability}

The published term uses one standard deviation for all $137$ days. Measured
against the truth on the tuning window the residual spread is
\SI{1.11}{\celsius} where the filter assumes \SI{0.69}{\celsius}, so the
temperature term is overconfident by a factor of $1.6$. More useful than the
average is that the tag predicts its own error. Sorted into quartiles of the
tag's own mixed-layer depth, the residual spread rises monotonically through
$0.54$, $0.75$, $1.07$ and \SI{1.69}{\celsius}; a variance model linear in
mixed-layer depth beats a constant one by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=-19.9$ on the
tuning window. Near-surface stratification does about as well and is
uncorrelated with calendar time ($r=-0.04$), so this is water-column physics and
not a drifting sensor. A competing hypothesis died cheaply: the animal reached
the surface on every single day the term fires, so a story about the tag never
getting shallow enough explains nothing.

The same geometry that makes a twilight bias unidentifiable applies here. The
meridional OISST gradient along this track is \SI{1.07}{\celsius} per degree of
latitude, so one degree of free thermometer bias buys \SI{103}{\km} of free
latitude. A latent temperature-bias state is therefore the
Section~\ref{sec:ident} trap in a different channel. A bias regressed on an
\emph{observed} covariate adds no latent freedom and is the only safe form, and
it is the only form tested below.

\subsection{Repairing it rotates the error instead of shrinking it}

Table~\ref{tab:sst} runs four versions of the temperature term through the same
forward filter. Against the published term, treating missing cells as
uninformative and taking the per-day standard deviation from the mixed-layer
depth improves the tuning-window mean by \SI{23}{\km}, the RMS by \SI{29}{\km}
and the $75$th percentile by \SI{82}{\km}, each past ten standard errors---while
making the median \SI{20}{\km} \emph{worse}. Both statements are true, and which
one is quoted is a decision about whether a typical day or a bad day matters
more. This paper has quoted the median almost everywhere, and the median is
precisely the statistic that cannot see a defect which ruins one day in five.

The signed error says why the median moved the wrong way. The median
tuning-window offset goes from \SI{33}{\km} south and \SI{70}{\km} west under the
published term to \SI{97}{\km} south and \SI{13}{\km} west under the fully
repaired one, while its magnitude stays between $70$ and \SI{98}{\km}. The error
does not shrink; it rotates from south-west to very nearly due south. Two defects
had been cancelling. Deleting every coastal cell acted as an offshore repulsion
that nobody wrote down, worth about \SI{25}{\km} of eastward error. The
unmodelled \SI{0.58}{\celsius} cold offset acted as a northward pull: at
\SI{1.07}{\celsius} per degree of latitude it should be worth \SI{60}{\km}, and
the observed loss of northward pull when it is modelled away is \SI{64}{\km}.
Repairing the measurement model honestly removes both accidental corrections and
exposes the southward twilight bias that Section~\ref{sec:ident} says cannot be
removed at all.

One further negative is worth recording. The per-day \emph{bias} term is
preferred by AIC on the observation residual and is worse than the per-day
standard deviation alone on every filter statistic in Table~\ref{tab:sst}.
Fitting the residual better is not the same thing as locating the animal better,
and an information criterion computed on the residual cannot distinguish them.

\subsection{What the repair is worth once you smooth}

Run through the smoother of Section~\ref{sec:smooth} instead of the filter, the
repair almost vanishes: tuning-window mean error moves from $94.4\pm3.7$ to
$92.5\pm1.2$\,\si{\km}, half a standard error, with the $75$th percentile
improving by $14.7\pm6.6$\,\si{\km} and the lockbox mean going the wrong way by
$20.2\pm9.5$\,\si{\km}. The filter needed the repair because it has only the past
to work with, and a day on which the truth is deleted is unrecoverable. A
smoother that can see both directions repairs those days by itself.

The conclusion for the temperature channel is therefore negative in the useful
sense. It held no untapped accuracy. What it held was an unflagged defect that
was making the filter wrong in a flattering direction, and the tag's own
depth--temperature profiles were enough to find it without a single new data
source. The repair should be adopted anyway, on the grounds that a likelihood
must not assign probability zero to a place the animal demonstrably was, and not
on the grounds that it improves a score.

\section{Process Noise and Habitat Priors}
\label{sec:prior}

The last item on the list was the motion model and a weak preference for
plausible habitat. I expected the smallest return of the three and got the
largest lockbox improvement in the project, attached to a result that does not
mean what it appears to mean.

\subsection{One day of data cannot find this animal}

Before adding a prior I wanted a number for how much the measurement model
knows on its own. Both checks below use the $176$ of $241$ events whose true
position can be defended by interpolation between Argos fixes less than four
days apart. The hard feasible set contains that position on $98.3\%$ of them,
so the constraint is not what is hurting.

The first check evaluates the filter's own measurement likelihood---the same
code, asserted line for line against the body of the filter---at the true
position and at the filter's answer. The answer scores higher than the truth on
$51$ to $70\%$ of events depending on the readout and the window, and the
median log-likelihood gap is $0.01$ to $0.22$. Being \SI{430}{\km} from the
animal costs about a tenth of a log unit.

The second check deletes the filter. On a $351 \times 351$ grid at
$0.1\degree$ spacing, $123{,}201$ cells masked to the feasible set, I evaluated
one solar day of twilight and temperature data at every grid point, for each of
the $104$ days with defensible truth. The best point on that map lies
\SI{372.2}{\km} from the animal on the tuning window and \SI{533.2}{\km} in the
lockbox. The truth sits in the top $1.6\%$ and $11.1\%$ of the map
respectively, and the set within two log units of the maximum covers $3.7$ to
$4.7\%$ of the map, several hundred kilometres across.

One day of data therefore rules out most of the ocean and locates nothing
inside what is left. Every kilometre of accuracy in Table~\ref{tab:main} comes
from the motion model stitching a hundred vague days together. A prior is not
competing with the data for influence; there is very little to compete with.

\subsection{The motion model was fitted on the month the animal was migrating}

$\sigma_v$ was set from net daily displacement over the calibration window.
Splitting the tuning-window Argos fixes at the end of that window shows what
that choice bought: median displacement is $68.1$\,\si{\km} per root-day over
the $12$ calibration pairs and $16.2$ over the remaining $34$, a factor of
$4.2$. The calibration window is exactly the stretch in which the animal ran
south from Monterey. Over the whole tuning window the figure is $23.8$, and in
the lockbox, which was not consulted, $28.5$.

A prior-predictive check makes the consequence concrete. Simulating the
published motion model freely across the $47$ real Argos gaps in the tuning
window gives a median displacement of \SI{78.4}{\km} against \SI{32.4}{\km}
observed: a ratio of $2.42$ and a two-sample Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic of
$0.349$ ($p < 10^{-4}$). The scale that reproduces the observed median is
$0.40$; at $0.5$ the ratio is $1.22$ and the test no longer rejects
($p = 0.083$). The published filter swims about two and a half times too hard.

Table~\ref{tab:sigv} sweeps the scale through the forward filter. Halving
$\sigma_v$ improves all four tuning-window statistics at once, so it is
selected without the lockbox being consulted, and it transfers: lockbox median
error falls from $411.8$ to $312.5$\,\si{\km}. This is the same failure as the
light threshold in Section~\ref{sec:recal}, a parameter fitted on a window
whose conditions do not hold for the rest of the deployment, in a parameter I
had not thought to check.

\subsection{A weak habitat prior, and the control that ruins it}

The prior is a histogram. I read the seafloor depth under each of the $91$
tuning-window Argos fixes---quartiles $100$, $331$, $789$, $1379$ and
$3165$\,\si{\m}, with $62.6\%$ of them over water shallower than
\SI{1000}{\m}---and binned $\log_{10}$ depth into $18$ bins of width $0.25$, of
which $9$ are occupied. Laplace smoothing, peak normalised to zero, added to
the log-likelihood with weight $\lambda$. Eighteen numbers, all of them from
the tuning window, and nothing in any of them about position.

At $\lambda = 1$ it improves all eight statistics at once
(Table~\ref{tab:habitat}): tuning median $118.5$ to $83.8$ and lockbox median
$433.2$ to $290.6$\,\si{\km}. That is the largest single lockbox gain anywhere
in this study, out of eighteen numbers.

It is also wrong. Keeping the weight and the machinery and changing only the
contents of the histogram, I inverted the preference so that the prior pushes
the animal towards exactly the depths it avoids, and separately shuffled the
bin heights at random five times. The inverted prior beats the correct one on
the lockbox median, $240.1$ against $290.6$\,\si{\km}. Three of the five
shuffled priors also beat the published filter by more than \SI{140}{\km}.
Whatever the depth term is supplying, it is not habitat knowledge.

\subsection{What the depth term is actually doing}

The mechanism is confinement. For each of the seven depth priors I measured the
fraction of the ocean it leaves at full weight---its peak-normalised value
summed over water cells, divided by the number of water cells---and the
position of its weighted centre of mass. Effective area ranges from $7.2\%$ to
$63.3\%$, and across the seven priors its correlation with lockbox median error
is $+0.951$. The correlation with how close the prior's centre of mass sits to
the animal is $+0.740$, but area and distance are themselves correlated at
$+0.880$ across these seven; holding distance fixed, the area correlation is
$+0.940$, and holding area fixed the distance correlation changes sign to
$-0.667$. Every one of these priors, including the two that help most, has its
centre of mass more than \SI{1000}{\km} from the animal's mean lockbox
position. They are not pointing at the animal. They are narrow, and against a
likelihood as flat as the one measured above, narrow is enough.

The same argument predicts that the two interventions should combine, since
tightening $\sigma_v$ confines in time and the depth term confines in space.
Table~\ref{tab:combo} shows that they do, and almost exactly additively:
together they give a lockbox median of $165.0 \pm 1.2$ and a lockbox mean of
$158.8 \pm 3.7$\,\si{\km}, against $433.2$ and $446.5$ for the published
filter. That is the best lockbox accuracy in the project by a factor of $2.6$.

\subsection{What I adopt, and what I only report}

Halving $\sigma_v$ is adopted. It is chosen by the tuning window on all four
statistics, it has a prior-predictive justification that does not reference
error at all, and it transfers.

The depth prior is reported and not adopted, because its own control beats it.
The combination is reported and not adopted for a different reason: on the
tuning window it has a $99$th percentile of \SI{872}{\km} and a worst day of
\SI{903}{\km}, and its tuning mean and RMS are worse than the published
filter's. It is excellent four days in five and badly wrong on the fifth.
Under this study's own rule---select on the tuning window, score the lockbox
once---it is rejected. The rule that stops me adopting it is the same rule that
would have stopped me ever finding it, which is a fair statement of what
held-out evaluation costs as well as what it buys.

If the measurement model is this flat, anything that shrinks the support will
look like skill. The only way I found to tell confinement from information was
to build a deliberately wrong version of the same term and check whether it
worked too.

\section{Does Any of This Transfer?}
\label{sec:cohort}

Everything above is one animal, which is the first thing anyone should attack.
The archive these data come from holds $70$ deployments, and seven of those
animals carried a PAT and a SPOT tag at the same time. Those seven are the only
animals in the database that can be scored the way 07\_05 was scored, because
they are the only ones with an independent position while the light tag was
running.

I ran the frozen configuration on all seven. The threshold $h_0$, the
attenuation coefficient $\beta$, the twilight noise model, $\sigma_v$ and the
halving adopted in Section~\ref{sec:prior} are exactly the values 07\_05's
tuning window produced. Three deployment facts change per animal---release
time, release location, and the animal's own archival depth record---and
nothing else. For the six new animals there is no tuning window at all: every
day is held out, and 07\_05 stays in the table as a control whose numbers must
not move.

\subsection{Two defects that one animal could not reveal}

The first run of this experiment produced a table I could not defend, and
chasing why exposed two bugs. Both were silent: no exception, no warning.

The seafloor constraint was the first. \texttt{ZWIN}, the per-twilight maximum
dive depth that makes a position admissible, was built once as a dictionary
keyed by 07\_05's timestamps. On any other animal every lookup missed, the
depth returned not-a-number, the test was skipped, and the feasible set
degraded silently to ocean-only. Measured directly, the lookup succeeded on
$95\%$ of 07\_05's twilights and on $0\%$ of every other animal's. The six new
animals were being scored under a weaker constraint than the animal they were
being compared against, and I would have reported the gap as a generalisation
result.

The release epoch was the second and the worse of the two. The filter starts
its clock at a module-level constant holding 07\_05's deployment date. 07\_03's
record ends two months \emph{before} that date, so every time increment came
out negative, the propagation block never executed, and the filter sat frozen
on its release prior for all $232$ of its twilights. A stationary estimate near
a release site scored $85$\,\si{\km}, the lowest number anywhere in this study,
and halving the process noise changed it by exactly zero---identical to fifteen
significant figures. That zero is the only reason I found the bug. The other
five animals each received one spurious first step of $135$ to $600$ days;
09\_15's $600$-day step alone accounted for a $1187$\,\si{\km} median and a
$483$\,\si{\km} seed spread.

Both repairs have the same shape as the release prior: swap a per-animal
deployment fact, touch no tuned parameter. The regression test is that 07\_05
rebuilt through the new per-animal path reproduces the original inline result
exactly---$241$ events against $241$, maximum absolute difference in observed
twilight hour $0$, and the same detachment timestamp---and that its scores are
unchanged to every digit in Table~\ref{tab:cohort}.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What survives}
A Student-$t$ twilight likelihood is worth about $100$\,\si{\km} and is the
largest single modelling win in this work --- considerably more than the choice
of filter, which is worth about $26$\,\si{\km}. Smoothing beats filtering
consistently for all three estimators, and a particle smoother is the single
largest improvement in the study: $119.0\pm1.4$ to $68.6\pm4.4$\,\si{\km} on the
tuning window at $10.9$ standard errors, with the posterior narrowing to $0.65$
of its former spread while coverage improves (Section~\ref{sec:smooth}). Independent aiding is what breaks the
equinox degeneracy: SST cuts March error from $522$ to $109$\,\si{\km}, because
its information is orthogonal to day length rather than because it is more
accurate. And once a hard constraint makes the posterior multimodal, the readout
stops being innocent: the same particle cloud reads $364$\,\si{\km} at the
posterior mean and $452$\,\si{\km} at the local MAP. Against four alternatives on
the same clouds --- marginal mode, weighted geometric median, highest-weight
particle and a single weighted draw --- the posterior mean is nonetheless the best
choice on the tuning window, and none of the four beats it out of sample by more
than its seed noise. One process-model parameter does survive on its own
terms: $\sigma_v$ was fitted on the calibration month, a prior-predictive check
at the real Argos gaps rejects it at $p<10^{-4}$, and halving it is chosen by
the tuning window on all four statistics and cuts held-out median error from
$412$ to $313$\,\si{\km} (Section~\ref{sec:prior}).

\subsection{What does not}
The headline, as a measure of accuracy. The ablation ladder, which was built
below the seed-noise floor. The raw-light recalibration, which improved the
quantity it was fitted to and tripled the held-out error. The bias-state fix, as an accuracy
claim, and in fact as any kind of estimate: Section~\ref{sec:ident} shows the
parameter was never identifiable from twilight data. And my own selection rule, as
a sufficient safeguard --- it is clean, and over a wider family it is actively
anti-correlated with accuracy. And the temperature channel, as a reservoir of further accuracy: Section~\ref{sec:sst} finds a real defect in it --- the satellite analysis has no value at the animal's own position on a fifth of the days the term fires, and the likelihood called those places impossible --- but repairing that defect rotates the error rather than reducing it, and buys nothing that survives smoothing. And the weak habitat prior, as habitat
knowledge: it produces the largest single held-out gain in the study and is then
beaten on that same window by a deliberately inverted copy of itself. Across
seven such priors, how much ocean each one deletes explains the gain
($r=+0.951$), and where each one points explains none of it once area is held
fixed (Section~\ref{sec:prior}).

\subsection{Limitations}
The diagnostic work is one animal, one deployment, and one ground-truth track;
only the process-noise result of Section~\ref{sec:cohort} has been tested on
more. The lockbox
contains $20$ matched events, so lockbox medians carry wide intervals --- the
seed standard deviation alone is $90$\,\si{\km}, and sampling error on $20$
events is comparable. Nothing here should be read as a general ranking of
nonlinear filters; it is a statement about what this data set can resolve, which
is less than I originally claimed. The Argos error model for classes 0, A and B
is assumed rather than known. GPE3 is a black box, so I can say that it
generalises better but not why.

\subsection{What I would do differently}
Hold out a block of ground truth before writing any model code --- a block at the
end, scored once. Report seed variance on every stochastic result and refuse to
interpret any difference smaller than it; two seeds would have killed most of the
ladder. And treat calibration support as a first-class check: for every fitted
sensor parameter, compare the range of its regressors in the calibration window
against the range expected at deployment. A goodness-of-fit plot cannot show you
an unidentifiable parameter; a support check can. I would also be slower to
reject a method on a back-of-envelope argument: I skipped backward simulation
because a correct calculation about weight concentration was attached to an
incorrect conclusion about ensemble collapse, and that one unchecked inference
cost about $50$\,\si{\km} (Section~\ref{sec:smooth}). Above all, before adding any
parameter to a state vector, count observations per epoch against unknowns per
epoch: that takes a minute and would have told me that the whole of
Section~\ref{sec:fix} was estimating a quantity the data cannot see. I would also report more than one error statistic. Almost every number here is a median, and a median cannot see a defect that ruins one day in five: the repair in Section~\ref{sec:sst} improves the mean by \SI{23}{\km} and the $75$th percentile by \SI{82}{\km} while making the median \SI{20}{\km} worse. And I would open every file in the archive before modelling rather than after; the evidence that the temperature term was broken had been sitting in two files the pipeline never read. And for anything that
improves a score by shrinking the posterior rather than by adding information,
I would build a deliberately wrong version of the same term and check whether it
works too; that one control is what separated Section~\ref{sec:prior}'s real
result from its apparent one.

\section{Conclusion}
Light-level geolocation of this record is dominated not by the choice of
nonlinear filter but by a biased measurement model that no filter in the family
can detect from the data it is given. On the window I tuned on, my best
configuration reaches $117$\,\si{\km} and approaches the manufacturer's
smoother. On 30 days I never looked at, it reaches $544$\,\si{\km} against the
smoother's $118$\,\si{\km}, and reports a $201$\,\si{\km} standard deviation
while being wrong by $532$\,\si{\km}.

The measurable contributions are therefore negative, and I think more useful than
the positive claims they replace. Seed-to-seed spread of $29$--$90$\,\si{\km}
invalidates an ablation ladder built from $1$--$5$\,\si{\km} steps.
Tuning-window rank predicts held-out rank only weakly ($\rho=+0.30$), so the
best-on-tuning variant ranks $54$th of $75$ out of sample. A recalibration that
halved its own fitting residual tripled held-out error, because the parameter it
adjusted was never identifiable from the calibration window. And a principled
one-parameter fix, chosen without ever touching the held-out data, improved
calibration and not accuracy --- and could not have improved accuracy, because a
twilight pair is two numbers, position is two unknowns, and a threshold bias is
therefore a linear combination of the two things it was meant to correct. The
same marginal-likelihood rule, given a three-dimensional bias family, prefers the
degenerate direction in every one of its top configurations and lands a factor of
four worse on the window it was computed on.

A smoother --- the one repair identifiability does not forbid --- then halves
tuning-window error while leaving held-out error statistically untouched, which
is the cleanest confirmation available that the residual failure is bias and not
variance.

The last two experiments make the same point from the other side. The
process-noise scale was fitted on the only month of the record in which the
animal was migrating; a prior-predictive check that never looks at error says
it is $2.4$ times too fast for the rest of the deployment, and halving it is
selected on tuning data and carries to the held-out window. A weak
seafloor-depth prior does better still, the largest single held-out gain
anywhere here, and then loses to an inverted copy of itself, because what it
supplies is not knowledge of where the animal is but a smaller region for it to
be in. When one solar day of data places its own best estimate
\SI{372}{\km} from the animal, almost anything that narrows the posterior will
look like skill. None of this says habitat information is worthless; it says
this particular prior earned its gain by shrinking the feasible area, so any
habitat term has to be scored against a deliberately wrong copy of itself
before it is credited with anything.

The estimator that generalises best here is still the proprietary one, and I
would rather treat that as the benchmark to beat than as a verdict on
light-based geolocation. The part of this
work I would defend is the protocol that let me find that out, and the part I
would keep is the habit of checking whether a parameter is estimable before
estimating it.

\section*{Reproducibility}
Everything runs top to bottom in a single Colab notebook with no manual steps.
The tag archives are fetched at run time from the ATN Data Assembly Center by
stable UUID, so a cold start gets byte-identical inputs; the raw data are not
redistributed. All seeds are fixed in code. Particle counts are stated with every
